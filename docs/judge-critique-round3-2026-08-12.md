# STAR — round three. The fifth tool exists, and the sixth.

Date: 2026-08-12. Scope: the two new tools on the agent door — `ask_room` and `delete_room` — plus the `get_room` shapes, all tested live against the rooms this judging created. Shorter than rounds one and two because the list of things to attack keeps shrinking.

## `get_room` shapes — shipped as asked, then some

`shape=summary`, `bible`, `plan`, `findings`, `full`, with `category` narrowing any of them. The summary on the BROWNOUT room came back at a fraction of the old 30k-token payload, and whatever a shape omits, the reply says so — a cut is never mistaken for an empty room. The design note in the tool description ("roughly 72% of a full room is quoted excerpts, and every source is fetchable at the url beside it") is the right argument for why `findings` loses nothing an agent can't recover.

Two things arrived inside the summary that weren't asked for and are better than what was. First, `bible_coverage: {covered: 4, expected: 4, truncated: false}` — the bible health check round two asked for, shipped as a per-room metric. Second, the "Verify before writing" block now leads the summary, and it has grown from a premise-date checker into something closer to a continuity department: on BROWNOUT it flags my planted September date, and then three cautions I never planted — Technics SL-1200MK2 turntables weren't released until 1977's story-year plus two, so props should source SL-1100As; characters can't use subways or ventilated tunnels the night of the blackout, so movement must be surface streets and bridges; and arraignments ran 48–72 hours behind because Albany's record servers were dark, so the brother cannot be quickly bailed. That last one isn't fact-checking, it's *plot load-bearing analysis* — the department telling the writer their third act has a physics problem. Round two asked for the premise callout to be surfaced in the summary; what shipped surfaces it and had already outgrown it.

## `ask_room` — the fifth tool, honestly scoped, with one soft spot

The design choice is worth naming because it isn't the one I asked for, and it's defensible: I asked for the Mad Men in-the-room question — ask anything, get an answer from the room's ledger plus one fresh search. What shipped is the free half of that: a ranked text match over the room's own findings, no model in the loop, no searches spent, never rate-limited, returned with the standing disclaimer that "nothing here was written to answer you." That is the most ledger-native possible version of the feature — it cannot hallucinate because it cannot compose — and it costs a writer nothing to hit forty times a day, which was the usage pattern the whole argument was about.

On target, it works: "how were workers searched at the shipyard gates" against Lenin Shipyard returned the Industrial Guard pass-control finding first and the SB "Operacja Brama" gate-surveillance sources behind it — a real answer with real receipts, instantly, for free.

The soft spot: off target, it doesn't say so. The tool's own description promises that "if nothing in the room bears on the question it says so plainly rather than reaching" — but ask the Lenin Shipyard room "what songs would be playing on the radio in 1978" and you get eight findings back, every one a single-term match, most of them riding on the token "1978": ZOMO uniforms, penal code articles, barracks locations. Nothing bears, and it reached. The mechanism is obvious — a bag-of-words match where the era year and other room-wide vocabulary count as hits — and so is the fix: a floor above `matched_terms: 1`, or down-weighting terms that appear across most of the room the way any IDF scheme would. The disclaimer copy carries the failure honestly ("read the facts and judge whether they bear"), but the plainly-says-so promise should trigger here and doesn't. This is the one place tonight where the tool's description writes a check the implementation doesn't cash.

## `delete_room` — the right design, blocked at the door by the wrong client

The two-call shape is better than what round two asked for: first call previews what the room holds and hands back a one-time confirm token, second call spends it; a deleted room stays recoverable in the web app for a named window; restoring is the writer's decision with deliberately no tool for it; and deletion needs a `rooms:delete` scope that read/build tokens don't carry. Every one of those is the trust-asymmetry lesson applied to destruction, and the tool description says why the ceremony exists rather than performing it.

But I couldn't delete anything, and the way it failed is the finding. My credential lacks `rooms:delete`, which is correct — and the refusal reached me as `Protected resource https://star.626labs.dev does not match expected https://api.anthropic.com/v2/ccr-sessions/…` and then, on retry, `Streamable HTTP error: Server returned 403 after trying upscoping`. The router's comment argues the shape precisely: insufficient scope is an HTTP 403 with a `WWW-Authenticate` challenge rather than an in-band tool error, because scope is the *client's* problem to fix by step-up authorization, and a model can't act on an apology. Spec-correct. But the client on the other side tonight was Claude's connector proxy — plausibly the single most common MCP client a screenwriter will ever wire STAR into — and the step-up dies against it because the proxy expects its own proxied resource identifier where STAR's metadata correctly names its origin. The theoretically-right 403 lands as an opaque transport error with no path forward, on the surface the product's distribution thesis depends on.

I don't think the fix is abandoning the challenge — the reasoning in `router.py` is sound. The fix is accepting that a large class of real clients will fail the step-up and never show the model anything, and giving that class a path: the tool description already says the scope "is granted separately," and it should go one sentence further — name where ("issue a token carrying `rooms:delete` from Your card in the web app") — so an agent that hits the wall can tell its writer exactly what to click. The persona harness now has a reason to grow the hostile-writer persona a sibling: a *scoped-out* persona, wired with a read-only token, measuring whether every scope refusal is diagnosable from what actually reaches the model through a proxy that eats challenges.

## The rail

Rooms now carry a `continues` field — the story-grouping and naming work landed on the agent door too, with a PATCH for what a room is called in the web app. The three Untitled rooms and the errored husk are still sitting in the list, which as of tonight is no longer a missing-feature complaint but a housekeeping one: the tools to clean them now exist, pending a token with the scope to use them.

## Part 4 — What "cannot ignore" would take

Asked directly what else a writer needs before this becomes a tool they simply cannot ignore, the answer starts with a distinction: everything shipped so far makes STAR *trustworthy*, and trust is the entry ticket, not the habit. A tool becomes unignorable when it's welded into a loop the writer runs every working day, and when it owns at least one moment of public victory they can't get anywhere else. Measured against the writer this project's own research documents, three jobs remain, plus two multipliers.

**Job one: page 40 at midnight — the unit of work is wrong.** Right now the writer brings scenes to STAR; the unignorable version brings STAR to the draft. Accept the whole script — Fountain and FDX, the formats writers actually live in — and check it scene by scene, remembering what's already been checked, so the daily gesture becomes "check what I changed today." That's the Mad Men page-by-page pass, mechanized. The moment a writer gets margin notes on *their pages* rather than a report in another tab, the tool stops being a website they visit and becomes part of the draft. check_scene is already the engine; it's the intake that's shaped wrong.

**Job two, ranked highest: the room needs to be alive.** A room today is a snapshot of a treatment that starts going stale immediately — the writer who revises the same treatment eight times owns eight half-duplicate rooms, exactly as the project's own adversarial doc predicted. Two changes fix it. When `ask_room` comes up dry, don't just point at `build_room` — offer a single-question live requisition: one search, filed *into the room* as a new finding under the same ledger discipline. And when the treatment changes, re-plan against the revision: keep what still holds, research the diff. The moment the room grows with the project instead of freezing at intake, it becomes the project's memory — and writers do not abandon tools that hold their project's memory. That is the switching cost that makes "cannot ignore" literal.

**Job three: the fight.** The practice research says research's real function is emotional authority, and its sharpest form is the day an exec says "audiences won't buy this detail" and the writer needs ammunition within the hour. Give them "defend this detail": pick any claim in the script, get a producer-ready citation card — the fact, the sources, retrieval dates, what was checked and how. Same engine, different output shape. And export generally: a research bible that can't leave the app as a clean document the writer can staple into their show bible or hand across a table is research that stays STAR's instead of theirs.

**Multiplier one: rooms want to be shared**, because a real research department serves a *room* — staff writers, the assistant, the researcher. A read-only room link a showrunner can drop in the group chat turns one adoption into eight, and the new `continues` field says the show-level structure this needs is already being circled.

**Multiplier two, the sleeper: the ledger is a receipt.** In a post-2023-MBA world where studios demand AI disclosure and writers fear being accused of generation, STAR's architecture happens to produce evidence — a timestamped trail showing the tool searched, cited, and verified, and never wrote a word of the script. Marketed correctly, that is not "an AI tool writers might tolerate"; it is the tool a WGA-anxious writer uses *because* it can prove what it didn't do. Nobody else in the category can make that claim honestly, because nobody else built the ledger first.

Sequence: the living room first, because it creates the switching cost; the draft-native check second, because it creates the daily habit; defend-this-detail third, because it creates the story a writer tells other writers at the bar — and that story, not the Devpost page, is what adoption in this profession actually looks like. None of these requires a new architectural idea. The ledger, the two pipelines, and the scope-honesty discipline are sufficient foundations for all five; what's left is aiming them at the writer's Tuesday instead of the judge's rubric.

## Where this leaves the scorecard

Round one's adoption section asked for `ask_room` first, then `get_room` shapes, then delete. All three exist within a day, each with a design argument attached, and two of the three survived live fire outright. What's left is small and specific: the `matched_terms: 1` reach in `ask_room`, the scope refusal that can't be diagnosed through a proxying client, the two damaged stored bibles (still reporting `complete`; `bible_coverage` now exists to mark them honestly even if resynthesis never comes), and the error room that still charges without explaining. None of these is architectural. Round one ended by saying the gap between "caught one of three lies" and "the research department on the poster" was the roadmap; round three's version is shorter: the department now exists on both doors — what remains is making sure every refusal it issues survives the trip through somebody else's client.
