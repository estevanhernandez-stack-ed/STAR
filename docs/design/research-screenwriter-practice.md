# How working screenwriters actually do world/period research

Research compiled via web search, August 2026. Every claim below is sourced; anything not directly
supported by a fetched source is explicitly labeled **[inference]**.

---

## 1. The real workflow

**Before writing.** Screenwriters read biographies, watch documentaries, study photographs, and work
from primary sources (letters, diaries, official records) alongside secondary sources (books, journal
articles). The stated goal is not literal truth but "emotional authenticity" — accuracy in service of
scenes, not accuracy for its own sake.
Source: [Now Trending! Stage 32 — Historical Accuracy in a Screenplay](https://www.stage32.com/lounge/screenwriting/Historical-Accuracy-in-a-Screenplay), [Arc Studio — Historical Drama Screenwriting](https://www.arcstudiopro.com/blog/historical-drama-screenwriting)

A recurring metaphor across multiple sources: research is "the foundation of the house that is the
screenplay — essential, but you rarely see it directly on the page." Writers describe wanting the
research to sit in their "psyche" so they're "living in" the period, rather than consulting it
mid-sentence.
Source: [Arc Studio Pro — Historical Drama Screenwriting](https://www.arcstudiopro.com/blog/historical-drama-screenwriting)

**A concrete phase model**, from a working "fact-based screenplay" advisor (Substack, industry-adjacent,
not a studio source but detailed and specific):
1. Gather broadly — diverse sources, no filtering yet.
2. Read wide for context and background.
3. Narrow to the specific details the narrative actually needs.

Research splits into two named tracks: **macro** (the wider story world — what era, what rules) and
**micro** (narrative-specific details needed for a given scene). The guiding question writers actually
ask themselves: *"what do I need to know to tell this story?"* — not "what is everything true about this
period."
Source: [Ten Minute Time Travel / fact-based screenplay Substack — How to research a fact-based screenplay](https://thefactbasedscreenplay.substack.com/p/how-to-research-a-fact-based-screenplay)

**During writing and in the room.** For TV, research is not a one-time front-load — it's continuous and
demand-driven. On *Mad Men*, head of research Kathryn Allison Mann reviewed every script draft
**page-by-page**, flagging anachronisms and errors, and fed notes to the writers' assistant, who folded
them into showrunner Matthew Weiner's rewrite pass. She also fielded live, ad-hoc questions from
writers **in the room** when a storyline idea came up ("was there a word for X yet," "would a woman have
had access to Y"), and built full **timelines** for complex episodes (e.g., the moon landing episode) as
a navigational aid, not a fact-dump.
Source: [TIME — As Show's Collections Go to Archive, 'Mad Men' Head of Research Shares Her Process](https://time.com/4634575/mad-men-researcher/), [Ransom Center Magazine — How Mad Men's Head of Research Mined the Past](https://sites.utexas.edu/ransomcentermagazine/2017/01/12/how-mad-mens-head-of-research-mined-the-past-for-the-shows-famous-historical-accuracy/)

On *The Crown*, the process is described as: writers (led by Peter Morgan) lay out plot and dialogue
first, and researchers **annotate the scripts afterward** with additional facts — sometimes broad,
sometimes minute ("how did they get there, who was on the plane, what was the boat called"). Crucially,
research is also used **generatively**, not just as a check: research head Annie Sulzberger says of her
team's job, "We show [Peter] these other events maybe he hasn't thought of before or were more under the
radar" — i.e., research surfaces story ideas the writer didn't know to look for, not just verifies ones
he already had.
Source: [Netflix Tudum — How accurate is The Crown? Go inside the show's royal research](https://www.netflix.com/tudum/articles/the-crown-season-5-accurate) (via search-result excerpt)

**Who does it at the studio/show level, and what they hand over.** Two distinct roles surface:

- **Writers' assistants** — a real, credited job in the writers' room hierarchy. They "take detailed
  notes of story breaks, pitches, character/story arcs," track **continuity**, conduct research, and
  format documents. Research is bundled into a broader documentation/continuity job, not a standalone
  research function, on most shows.
  Source: [Final Draft — Who's in a TV Writers Room? Roles and Jobs Explained](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/whos-in-a-tv-writers-room-roles-and-jobs-explained)

- **Dedicated researcher / head of research** — exists on well-funded historical/prestige shows (*Mad
  Men*, *The Crown*). This is a named, non-writing staff position that sits between the writers' room
  and outside experts.
  Source: [TIME](https://time.com/4634575/mad-men-researcher/), [Tudum](https://www.netflix.com/tudum/articles/the-crown-season-5-accurate)

- **Historical consultants** — outside freelance experts (often academics) brought in per-project,
  advising on costume, set, dialect, and content. Named examples: Robert Lacey (*The Crown*, *The
  Queen*), Kathleen Coleman (*Gladiator*), Orlando Figes (*Anna Karenina*). Consultants report tension
  between authenticity-of-detail and "truth to the spirit" of the past — i.e., even the experts don't
  agree that literal accuracy is the goal.
  Source: [Luke McKernan — Film and the historian](https://lukemckernan.com/2018/11/19/film-and-the-historian/)

- **Working freelance historical consultants for hire** (a newer, more accessible tier — e.g.
  "SceneSpan," "Ten Minute Time Travel") deliver **curated research packages**: primary + secondary
  sources, distilled reading, and — this is the load-bearing phrase — **"scene-level cues from real
  archival sources,"** explicitly organized around emotional core rather than a timeline of facts.
  Source: [Ten Minute Time Travel — How I Approach Historical Consulting for Film, TV and Novels](https://tenminutetimetravel.substack.com/p/how-i-approach-historical-consulting)

**Production design does its own, parallel research track**, distinct from the writers'/story research
above — visual and material rather than narrative. Designers read the script multiple times capturing
"a single word, emotion, or quotation," then move outward: galleries, museums, archives, art-bookshops,
contemporary-witness interviews, photographs — with **the internet treated explicitly as "last resort,
as it is the easiest approach."** Findings get organized into folders by "themes, tones and character,"
mood boards, and shared via Dropbox; printed material gets physically posted in the office to influence
other departments.
Source: [Production Designers Collective — How do you conduct historical research?](https://www.productiondesignerscollective.org/how-do-you-conduct-historical-research/)

## 2. Real artifacts, and what they're actually called

- **Show bible / series bible** — these two terms are used **interchangeably** in the industry; there is
  no meaningful distinction between them. A bible (sometimes "pitch bible") is the reference document
  covering characters, backstories, settings, world rules, and arcs across a season/series. Typical
  length cited: **15–30 pages**. Its stated job, bluntly: "make someone who reads dozens of pitches a
  week believe your show deserves to exist for five seasons" — i.e., it's a **selling document** as much
  as a reference document, at least in its pitch-phase form.
  Source: [StudioBinder — What is a Show Bible](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/what-is-a-show-bible-examples-template/), [No Film School — Show Bible Template](https://nofilmschool.com/show-bible-template)

- **Beat sheet** — real, precise, and *not* the same thing as a research document or an outline. A beat
  sheet is a skeletal list of the story's major emotional/structural turning points ("beats") — pacing
  and turns, not scene-by-scene detail, not facts. An **outline** is the more granular, scene-by-scene
  document. Writers commonly use both, but neither is where research facts live.
  Source: [Save the Cat — A Writer's Guide to Beats and Beat Sheets](https://savethecat.com/about-the-beats/a-writers-guide-to-beats-and-beat-sheets), [The Script Lab — What is a Beat Sheet?](https://thescriptlab.com/blogs/39527-what-is-a-beat-sheet/)

- **Lookbook** — real term, but it's the **production design/cinematography/costume** artifact, not the
  writers' research artifact. It's a curated set of images/stills/color references/wardrobe and
  architecture inspiration used to align department heads on visual tone *before* production. It is
  explicitly visual-first and largely textless — "show, don't tell" as its own design principle.
  **[Inference]**: if STAR's UI borrows the word "lookbook" for a prose-and-citation research bible, it
  will read as mislabeled to anyone from production design, because a lookbook is images-and-mood, not
  cited facts.
  Source: [No Film School — How to Make a Lookbook for Your Film or TV Project](https://nofilmschool.com/film-lookbook), [Go Make a Scene — Lookbook dictionary entry](https://gomakeascene.com/resources/showbiz-dictionary/planning-and-pre-production/lookbook/)

- **World bible / universe bible** — used specifically for franchise/IP work (multi-installment,
  multi-medium). Distinguished from a show bible by scope: it's written from a "real-world" production
  guideline perspective (what's permitted/forbidden across future stories) more than in-universe lore.
  Source: [Laterpress — How and Why to Use a Series Bible as a Writer](https://www.laterpress.com/craft-of-writing/series-bible/)

- **Research notes** — the actual term the *Mad Men* head of research used for her per-draft deliverable
  to the writers' assistant: not a bible, not a report — "notes," page-by-page, tied to a specific script
  draft. **[Inference]**: this is the closer real-world analog to what STAR calls a "research bible" —
  but note it was per-draft and disposable/iterative, not a single static reference document.
  Source: [TIME — Mad Men researcher](https://time.com/4634575/mad-men-researcher/)

- **Continuity** — a tracked, named responsibility (assigned to writers' assistants and script
  coordinators), distinct from "research" as a category. Continuity is about internal consistency across
  episodes/drafts (did we already establish this character was in London that week), not factual
  grounding against the outside world. STAR's four categories don't currently have a continuity-tracking
  analog — **[inference]** this may be a gap or a deliberately excluded scope.
  Source: [Final Draft — Who's in a TV Writers Room](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/whos-in-a-tv-writers-room-roles-and-jobs-explained)

## 3. Tools they already live in

- **Final Draft** — industry-standard script-formatting tool; has a **"beat board"** and **story map**
  feature for outlining during rewrites, with color-coding. Not primarily a research tool.
  Source: [via WriterDuet/Final Draft comparison search](https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/best-screenwriting-software-78776/)

- **WriterDuet** — cloud-based, real-time collaborative screenwriting tool. Has built-in **index
  cards** (auto-generated into a separate "Cards" panel), a **"Private Pad"** for personal notes, and
  virtual corkboard-style cards with color/tag/size customization. This is the closest mainstream
  screenwriting tool to a "digital corkboard."
  Source: [G2 — WriterDuet Reviews](https://www.g2.com/products/writerduet/reviews)

- **Scrivener** — a writer favorite (not exclusive to screenwriters) for its **Corkboard view**: index
  cards representing scenes/chapters, draggable to reorder, plus a dedicated **research pane/binder**
  for storing notes, images, and references *alongside* the manuscript rather than in a separate app.
  This "manuscript and research live in one binder" model is a loved pattern.
  Source: [Literature & Latte — Organize Your Scrivener Project with the Corkboard](https://www.literatureandlatte.com/blog/organize-your-scrivener-project-with-the-corkboard)

- **Physical index cards** — still real practice among prominent screenwriters (e.g., John August),
  used to represent one scene/sequence per card, laid out on a table or corkboard and physically
  rearranged. The tactile, spatial rearrangement is the point — not just information storage.
  Source: [ScreenCraft — Hollywood Screenwriter John August's 10 Best Index Card Practices](https://screencraft.org/blog/hollywood-screenwriter-john-augusts-10-best-index-card-practices/) (title/topic confirmed via search; full text fetch failed — DNS error — so treat specifics as **[inference from title + search snippet only]**)

- **Milanote** — explicitly described as a **digital corkboard**: pin notes, images, links, files in
  freeform arrangement. Loved for visual/moodboard-style planning (character webs, references, themes);
  explicitly *not* recommended by comparison articles when a project needs "advanced project management
  features, complex databases, or extensive automation" — i.e., its strength (freeform, visual,
  lightweight) is also its ceiling.
  Source: [Toolstack — Milanote Review](https://toolstack.io/tools/milanote), search-result summary

- **Notion** — sentiment is genuinely split, not uniformly loved. Praised for flexibility ("a digital
  binder you can bend into any shape") and long-form project tracking. Criticized as "for everyone and
  no one at the same time, incredibly easy to shoot yourself in the foot with," "way too slow," "painful
  overkill," and "too overloaded" by some writer-users; its template system is called "a source of great
  confusion" for others. **Structure-heavy, flexible tools earn resentment as often as loyalty** among
  this user base — a real risk if STAR's UI leans toward a database/table-heavy Notion-like structure.
  Source: [Indie Hackers thread + Jon Howski Substack, via search summary](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/why-is-everyone-so-obsessed-with-notion-lately-1ddcb64f80)

## 4. Native vocabulary

Confirmed as real, working terms (not film-school-only):

- **Anachronism** — a detail (object, slang, custom, event) placed in the wrong time period. Used both
  for unintentional errors ("flubs") and deliberate artistic choices (e.g., *Walker*'s Coke cans and
  Apple computers as a political device). This is the single most load-bearing word in the whole space —
  it is the *name of the failure mode* research exists to prevent.
  Source: [LiteraryTerms.net](https://literaryterms.net/anachronism/), [The Spool — Alex Cox on Walker](https://thespool.net/interviews/alex-cox-interview-walker/)

- **Verisimilitude** — "lifelikeness," the believability of the fictional world; from Latin *verum*
  (truth) + *similis* (similar). Explicitly linked to anachronism in the literature: "unintended
  anachronisms undermine the verisimilitude that screenwriters work to maintain." This is a real working
  term, not exclusively academic — but it reads more formal/craft-book than something a writer would
  bark out mid-rewrite. **[Inference]**: the plainer working synonym writers reach for is "pulled me out
  of it" / "broke the spell," not the Latin word.
  Source: [Storm Writing School — Verisimilitude](https://stormwritingschool.com/verisimilitude/)

- **Grounded / grounding** — recurs independently across *three separate, unrelated sources* (Mad Men
  researcher, The Crown researcher, production designers) as the term for a detail that makes a scene
  feel real: "minute details that allow a scene to feel more grounded" (Crown); designers pursuing "a
  sense of reality." This convergence across otherwise-unconnected interviews is a strong signal that
  **"grounding" / "grounded" is the actual native word for what STAR calls a fact that "grounds a
  scene."**
  Source: [Netflix Tudum](https://www.netflix.com/tudum/articles/the-crown-season-5-accurate), [Production Designers Collective](https://www.productiondesignerscollective.org/how-do-you-conduct-historical-research/)

- **Primary source / secondary source** — real, actively used distinction, and explicitly a **trust**
  distinction, not just an academic taxonomy: "primary sources are unfiltered, while secondary sources
  are filtered." Writers and consultants alike reach for this language unprompted.
  Source: [Ten Minute Time Travel — How I Approach Historical Consulting](https://tenminutetimetravel.substack.com/p/how-i-approach-historical-consulting)

- **Continuity** — internal-consistency tracking (did we already say this happened), a distinct concept
  from factual/historical accuracy, owned by writers' assistants/script coordinators as a named job
  function.
  Source: [Final Draft — Who's in a TV Writers Room](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/whos-in-a-tv-writers-room-roles-and-jobs-explained)

- **Breaking story** / **the room** — the vocabulary of the collaborative plotting process itself
  ("breaking" an episode's acts and beats, done "in the room"). Not directly about research, but frames
  *when* research questions get asked — live, conversationally, mid-break, not as document review.
  Source: [The Script Lab — 13 TV Writers' Room Terms](https://thescriptlab.com/features/screenwriting-101/8890-13-tv-writers-room-terms-you-should-know/)

- **Story spark** — a real term from the fact-based-screenplay research literature: a research finding
  (a character moment, quote, or detail) worth capturing specifically *because* it suggests a scene —
  i.e., research that generates story rather than just supporting it. Pairs with "tentpole" — a
  load-bearing real fact around which a dramatized scene is built.
  Source: [Ten Minute Time Travel — How to research a fact-based screenplay](https://thefactbasedscreenplay.substack.com/p/how-to-research-a-fact-based-screenplay)

- **Historicism** (used pejoratively by critics) — excessive, showy focus on period detail for its own
  sake, at the expense of story. A useful negative-space term: it names the failure mode of *too much*
  research surfacing in the final work, the mirror image of anachronism.
  Source: [Research in Film and History — The Authenticity Feeling](https://film-history.org/approaches/authenticity-feeling)

Terms searched for but **not found as established working vocabulary** (treat as film-school or
copywriting invention if used in UI copy): "receipts," "paper trail," "source it" as screenwriter slang
for citations — no evidence found despite direct search.
Source: search returned no matches; [ScreenCraft — Industry Lingo](https://screencraft.org/blog/industry-lingo-every-screenwriter-needs-to-know-to-succeed/) and [LeeJessup — Industry Speak](https://leejessup.com/industry-speak-terms-and-vernacular-for-screenwriters/) exist as vocabulary references but did not surface these specific terms.

## 5. What makes handed-over research trusted or distrusted

- **Primary vs. secondary is the explicit trust axis.** A working historical consultant states it
  directly: primary sources (letters, diaries, first-person archival material) are "unfiltered"; secondary
  sources (biographies, popular histories) are "filtered" — i.e., already interpreted by someone else,
  and therefore one step further from ground truth. The stated risk of relying only on biographies:
  "you'll only see your subject through the lens of their biographer... you'll never hear from them, in
  their own unfiltered words." **Trust tracks distance from the original voice/document, not just
  correctness.**
  Source: [Ten Minute Time Travel — How I Approach Historical Consulting](https://tenminutetimetravel.substack.com/p/how-i-approach-historical-consulting)

- **Cross-referencing multiple sources, and preferring "the most definitive, reliable source" or "the
  most definitive expert"** is stated explicitly as the verification method by the Mad Men researcher —
  not one citation, a corroborating set.
  Source: [TIME — Mad Men researcher](https://time.com/4634575/mad-men-researcher/)

- **Distrust of dramatized/secondhand history.** Multiple independent sources flag the same failure
  mode: relying on "dramatised versions of history" (i.e., other movies/shows about the period) rather
  than original sources. This is presented as a rookie mistake, not a neutral shortcut.
  Source: [Ten Minute Time Travel — Incorrect Research Can Undermine Your Creative Vision](https://tenminutetimetravel.substack.com/p/incorrect-research-can-undermine)

- **Historical consultants themselves are treated with some skepticism industry-wide** — multiple
  named consultants (Robert Lacey, Orlando Figes, Kathleen Coleman) describe "mixed experiences" and
  argue for a middle ground between literal authenticity and dramatic truth, implying friction even
  between professional historians and the productions that hire them; consultants don't always get final
  say and are sometimes overridden for story reasons.
  Source: [Luke McKernan — Film and the historian](https://lukemckernan.com/2018/11/19/film-and-the-historian/), [Variety — Rise of TV Period Dramas Creates Bigger Need for History Consultants](https://variety.com/2019/artisans/production/tv-period-dramas-history-consultants-1203173244/)

- **Emotional/scene-relevance over encyclopedic completeness builds trust; a data-dump does not.** The
  consultant source is explicit that the valuable deliverable is "scene-level cues from real archival
  sources" focused on emotional core — not a timeline of facts. **[Inference]**: a research bible
  organized as an exhaustive fact-list, without a clear line to "here's the scene this could unlock,"
  will read to a screenwriter as generic/untrustworthy busywork rather than usable research, regardless
  of citation quality.
  Source: [Ten Minute Time Travel — How I Approach Historical Consulting](https://tenminutetimetravel.substack.com/p/how-i-approach-historical-consulting)

- **AI-generated research specifically carries a documented, current trust problem** — general (not
  screenwriting-specific) 2025–2026 sources report frontier LLMs still hallucinating on 15–20% of factual
  citation tasks, rising to 35–55% on niche/recent topics, and that models present wrong answers "cloaked
  in polished, hyper-confident language," which makes errors *harder* to catch, not easier, the more
  fluent the output looks. **No screenwriting-specific source was found** addressing AI research trust
  directly — this is general-domain evidence, extrapolated. **[Inference]**: given the strong
  primary-source bias documented above, a screenwriter's default posture toward an AI-authored "cited"
  document is likely to be *higher* scrutiny than toward a human researcher's notes, not lower — the
  UI should probably make verification/source-checking easier and more prominent than a typical SaaS
  citation footnote would, precisely because confident AI prose is the known failure mode.
  Source: [Axios — AI is still getting things wrong, more confidently than ever](https://www.axios.com/2026/05/30/ai-accuracy-chatbots-hallucinations), [general hallucination-rate studies via search summary](https://www.getmaxim.ai/articles/the-state-of-ai-hallucinations-in-2025-challenges-solutions-and-the-maxim-ai-advantage/)

## Notes on source quality

Most of this was gathered via WebSearch result summaries (which are themselves AI-generated syntheses of
search snippets) plus a handful of direct WebFetch reads of full articles (Mad Men/TIME, Mad Men/Ransom
Center, Production Designers Collective, fact-based-screenplay Substack, historical-consulting Substack,
"Incorrect Research" Substack, StudioBinder show-bible piece, Final Draft writers'-room piece, and the
film-history.org authenticity piece). Two fetches failed outright (ScreenSkills researcher profile — 403;
screencraft.org index-card article — DNS error) and are flagged inline above where their content is used.
The Netflix Tudum "Crown" research material came through as a search-engine synthesis, not a direct
fetch — flagged inline. Treat single-source claims with appropriate caution; claims that recur across
independently-sourced material (e.g., "grounded/grounding," primary-vs-secondary trust) are more solid.
