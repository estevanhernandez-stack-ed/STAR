# vibe-glow dogfood — STAR, 2026-08-11

**Destination:** this belongs in the vibe-glow repo at `docs/dogfood/star-2026-08-11.md`.
It was written into the target repo because **vibe-glow's source repo is not on

entry for it. Move it when the repo is to hand.

**Campaign:** app-wide, adopted identity (`DIRECTION.md`), one `default` theme.
**Result:** 25 findings, 25 clean, 7 waves, 606 → 623 tests.

## What the audit missed

The audit was thorough on its own terms — 67 lens findings, 67 adversarial
skeptics, 21 survivors, and the skeptics recomputed ratios from hex and drove a
live browser rather than voting. It still missed things, and the pattern in what
it missed is the useful part.

**The most visually dominant element on the primary evidence surface.** `F-025`
was filed by a *build*, not by the audit: a grep of the finished register for
"excerpt" returned **zero hits across all 67 findings**. The citation card's
excerpt block was 82% of the card and rendered raw scraped markdown as literal
characters. Five lenses looked at that card. None looked at the biggest thing on
it. **Hypothesis for the next hardening pass:** lenses inspect *components* and
this was *content* — payload-shaped rather than CSS-shaped — so it fell between
the visual lens (which reads layout) and the copy lens (which reads strings the
app authors). Nothing owned strings the app merely *displays*.

**Three findings that only appeared once someone was inside the code.** `F-022`,
`F-023` and `F-024` were all filed mid-build under the scope law. Two of them are
the same mechanism a wave had just fixed one surface over. An audit reading
source cannot see "and the identical bug is three files away" as reliably as a
builder who just fixed it.

**A rule that was sound but unevidenced.** Rule 9 (state a scope obligation once
per surface) survived all 67 adversarial reviews with **no confirmed violation** —
every finding citing it was correctly refuted — while the builder could see at a
glance that the copy read forced. The register had no rule for *form*, so nothing
could be filed against it. Rule 11 was added mid-campaign and immediately
licensed a bad proposal, which three independent reviews then killed; the rule
itself had to be amended ("a mark is derived, never authored"). **A new rule
introduced mid-campaign should be treated as unproven until a wave has built
against it.**

## What the waves fought

**Fix directions were wrong far more often than expected.** Roughly two in five
of the rows I built had a fix direction that could not be followed as written.
Not vague — *wrong*. `F-006` asked for a shared `scrollBehavior()` helper that a
focus-driven scroll cannot consume (`focus()` takes no `behavior` option), and
for a "named heading" the surface does not have. `F-010`'s headline claimed every
failure message in the app and missed two. `F-009` said a ratio was "unrecorded
anywhere" when a prior wave had recorded it. **The waves only worked because the
briefs re-verified every claim against the code before building.** A campaign
that trusts its own register ships the register's mistakes.

**Source assertions cannot see an accessibility tree.** The sharpest lesson of
the campaign. `F-006` shipped a `region` that was **completely unnamed** while
passing all 18 source assertions written for it, because Chromium computes no
accessible name when `aria-labelledby` points at a *descendant* of the region it
names. Only reading the live tree caught it. Every a11y claim afterwards was
settled in a browser.

**Tests that stop guarding without failing.** Four separate instances:
- a `z-index` assertion that ran against a whole stylesheet, where a different
  rule's `z-index: 0` satisfied it;
- a 2.5° rotation ceiling checked against one file, which would have gone
  **quietly green** the moment the rule it governs moved to another file — which
  the very next wave did;
- an assertion satisfied by a variable's own `let` declaration;
- an assertion satisfied by a second, unrelated call elsewhere in the file.

All four were caught by *mutation*, none by reading. **Recommendation: make
"prove the test fails when the bug is reintroduced" a stated wave requirement,
not a habit.** It was the single highest-yield practice in this campaign.

**Two environment traps, hit repeatedly.**
- **Stale cached assets, three times.** The app serves an ETag and no
  `Cache-Control`, so the browser reuses modules without revalidating. Twice a
  correct fix looked broken; once a whole surface appeared unchanged after a
  successful build. Cost real diagnosis time each time. **A capture or a live
  check during a glow campaign should cache-bust by default.**
- **CRLF, twice.** Working copies are CRLF on Windows; scripts wrote LF. A test
  passed on a branch and failed on `main` on the same commit. One of those went
  out as a red `main` because a verification command piped pytest into `tail`,
  and the pipe handed the chain `tail`'s exit code.

## What the adapter could not capture

`web-playwright`, and these are limits rather than bugs.

- **Wave 3 is invisible to it entirely.** Live-region absence, focus loss and
  announcement order are not screenshot-shaped and not machine-checkable. Five
  findings survived a 67-finding audit for exactly that reason. Screenshots were
  useless; reading the accessibility tree and pressing Tab were everything.
- **Element screenshots do not inherit the viewport**, which produced one
  round-1 capture at 768x405 instead of 1440x900, and **clip absolutely
  positioned content** — the drawer's cut tab sits above its card's box, so an
  element shot of the card cannot show the tab at all. Geometry had to stand in
  for the picture.
- **`fullPage: true` is a no-op** when the app scrolls an inner container rather
  than the document. Five early captures were mid-scroll before this was
  understood.
- **Whether a screen reader speaks any of it** remained unverifiable for the
  whole campaign. Every a11y close-out says so explicitly. That is the honest
  ceiling of this adapter and worth stating in the plugin's own docs.

## What worked, and should be kept

- **The scope law** ("a wave ships only its findings; anything found becomes a
  register addition"). It held every time the fix was one line, and it produced
  three real findings that would otherwise have been silent drive-bys.
- **Close-outs that hunt for what the wave broke.** Three of seven found a defect
  the wave itself introduced, including one where a wrong number sat inside the
  paragraph written to correct a wrong number.
- **Re-deriving the scoreboard from the register's rows at reveal**, not from
  wave summaries. It was clean here, but it is cheap and it is the one check that
  cannot be self-confirming.
- **Recording the pixel that moved.** Two "byte-identical" merges turned out to
  contain values that had already drifted 0.05rem apart; saying "this moves the
  rail 0.8px" is worth more than a claim of no-op that is 95% true.
